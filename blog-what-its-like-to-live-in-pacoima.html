What It Is Really Like to Live in Pacoima

October 22, 2026

Some neighborhoods in Los Angeles feel like they were built for whoever arrives next. Pacoima feels like the opposite. This is a place built by the families who have lived here for decades, who raised children and then grandchildren on the same blocks, who painted the murals, opened the shops, coached the teams, and kept the traditions going through every change the city sent their way. To live in Pacoima, in the northeast San Fernando Valley between San Fernando and Lake View Terrace, is to live somewhere with a memory, and that memory is present on almost every street.

A Community With Deep Roots

Pacoima is one of the oldest neighborhoods in the San Fernando Valley, with a history that reaches back long before the freeways, to the Indigenous village that gave the area its name and to the farming community that followed. In the twentieth century it became home to working families from many backgrounds, and in the postwar years it grew into a largely Latino, family centered neighborhood with a strong Black history as well, including residents who built lives here when other parts of the Valley were closed to them.

That layered history is not kept in a museum. It lives in family stories, in church congregations that have served the same blocks for generations, in alumni of San Fernando High School who still show up for games, and in local organizations that work on everything from youth programs to keeping the boulevards beautiful. Pacoima Beautiful, the community environmental organization founded here in the 1990s, is known across the city for its tree plantings, cleanups, and youth leadership work.

Van Nuys Boulevard And The Murals

Van Nuys Boulevard is the spine of daily life in Pacoima, running through the heart of the neighborhood with shops, restaurants, services, markets, and bus stops that keep it busy from morning to night. This is where people get haircuts, buy quinceanera dresses, pick up groceries, stop for tacos, and run into people they know at almost every stop.

Along the boulevard and on nearby walls is the stretch many people call Mural Mile. Pacoima has one of the highest concentrations of murals in Los Angeles, with large, vivid works honoring cultural heroes, local history, music, faith, and community pride. The murals are not decoration added later for visitors. Many were painted by local artists, with local permission, about local figures and stories, and residents can tell you who painted what and when. Driving or walking the boulevard becomes a kind of outdoor gallery tour, but one that belongs first to the people who live here.

Family Life On Residential Blocks

Off the main boulevards, Pacoima is mostly single family homes on modest lots, with duplexes and apartment buildings mixed in, many dating from the postwar building boom. What stands out is how fully the homes are lived in. Front yards have gardens and fruit trees. Backyards have patios built for gatherings, grills that get used most weekends, and in many cases additional living space for extended family, because multigenerational living is normal here, not an exception.

Grandparents helping raise grandchildren, adult children living close to or with parents, cousins a few blocks apart, this pattern shapes the neighborhood social life. Child care is often family care. Holidays are large. Help in an emergency is usually a short walk away. For families who value that closeness, Pacoima offers something increasingly rare in Los Angeles, an affordable feeling of rootedness where staying near family is still possible and still expected.

Hansen Dam And Open Space Nearby

One of the great everyday advantages in Pacoima sits just to the east. The Hansen Dam Recreation Area, in neighboring Lake View Terrace, is a large flood control basin and parkland along the Tujunga Wash that gives northeast Valley residents the kind of open space most city neighborhoods can only wish for. There are sports fields, picnic areas, an aquatic center with a large recreational lake and water features in season, equestrian trails, natural areas for walking and bird watching, and the Hansen Dam Golf Course.

For Pacoima families, Hansen Dam is where soccer leagues play, where birthday picnics spread across the grass, where early morning walkers get real distance in, and where horseback riding remains part of ordinary local life. The scale of it matters. Children grow up with a horizon. Teenagers have somewhere to go that is not a parking lot. Adults decompress after work with mountains in view, the San Gabriel Mountains rising close and dramatic to the northeast.

Closer to home, Ritchie Valens Park on Laurel Canyon Boulevard, named for the Pacoima born rock and roll pioneer, offers fields, courts, and a recreation center, and David M. Gonzales Recreation Center on Van Nuys Boulevard serves generations of local kids with sports and programs. These parks are social centers as much as green space, places where coaches and recreation staff know families by name.

Schools, Faith, And Local Institutions

Daily life in Pacoima runs through its schools and churches. Local elementary schools, Pacoima Charter and other campuses, Maclay Middle School nearby in San Fernando, and San Fernando High School form a shared experience across generations of families. Parents who attended a school often send their children there, and teachers may teach siblings years apart. That continuity cuts both ways at times, but residents value being known.

Faith communities are central, with Catholic parishes, evangelical and Pentecostal congregations, and smaller churches anchoring blocks with services in English and Spanish, food distributions, youth

groups, and celebrations through the year. The annual holiday traditions, posadas in December, community parades, and cultural festivals draw crowds that feel more like family reunions than public events.

Getting Around And The Honest Tradeoffs

Pacoima sits near the junction of major routes. The 5 freeway runs along its western edge, the 118 crosses its northern side, and the 405 and 210 are within a short drive, putting Burbank, Glendale, Santa Clarita, and downtown within commuting range, with the usual Los Angeles caveats about traffic. Metro bus lines on Van Nuys and Laurel Canyon boulevards connect residents to the Van Nuys station and the wider Valley, and the planned light rail line along Van Nuys Boulevard, the East San Fernando Valley Light Rail project, is expected to reshape transit here in the coming years with stations serving Pacoima directly.

Honesty matters when describing life here. Some industrial blocks sit close to homes, a legacy of older zoning. Some streets carry heavy truck and commuter traffic. Summer heat in the northeast Valley is serious, and shade and air conditioning shape summer routines. Residents do not pretend otherwise. What they will tell you is that the strengths are equally real, community, culture, family closeness, and a cost of staying that has allowed generations to remain together when other neighborhoods pushed families apart.

Why People Stay, And Why People Look Closer

People stay in Pacoima because they are known here. Their history is in the mural, the school photo, the church pew, the team roster. Their parents are close, their children have cousins nearby, and the restaurant on the boulevard knows their order. That web of belonging is the real infrastructure of the neighborhood, and it is why departures are often reluctant and returns are common.

When that understanding turns into a wish to put down roots here or nearby in the northeast Valley, local knowledge matters enormously, street by street and block by block. In a place like Pacoima, that respect is not a courtesy. It is the starting point.
