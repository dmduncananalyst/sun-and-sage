Los Angeles Measure ULA and Home Sale Costs

Measure ULA adds a City of Los Angeles transfer tax to qualifying higher-value real estate transfers. For closings after June 30, 2026, the City's published thresholds are more than $5.4 million for the 4% tier and $10.9 million or more for the 5.5% tier. Sellers should include any applicable transfer tax in their estimated net proceeds before making pricing or offer decisions.

The property's jurisdiction matters

A Los Angeles mailing address does not necessarily place a property within Los Angeles city limits. The city tax rules apply to transfers of real property within the City of Los Angeles, so jurisdiction is the first question to resolve. Some properties marketed as being in Los Angeles are in neighboring incorporated cities or unincorporated areas with different rules. A parcel's actual legal location, not the way an advertisement describes its neighborhood, determines whether the city framework should be evaluated. Before applying any estimate, confirm the address and parcel against authoritative jurisdiction information.

Two tax layers can affect a sale

Los Angeles has a base real property transfer tax of $2.25 per $500, or part of $500, of value transferred, which the Office of Finance describes as a base rate equivalent to 0.45%. Measure ULA adds a separate 4% or 5.5% tax for transfers in the applicable tiers. The published rate table therefore shows a combined nominal 4.45% in the first Measure ULA tier and 5.95% in the higher tier. The base-tax calculation includes rounding to the next $500 increment where needed. These are taxes on the qualifying transfer value, not taxes calculated only on the equity left after the mortgage is paid.

A sale price is not the amount the seller receives

The final proceeds calculation begins with the contract price and then considers loan payoff, closing costs, applicable transfer taxes, negotiated concessions and any other transaction-specific obligations. A seller considering two different offers should compare the estimated net amount after these items, not simply the higher number on the first page. Timing can also matter if thresholds change between planning and closing. A preliminary worksheet can help frame the discussion, but it should not be represented as the closing statement or as individualized tax advice.

Exemptions require a genuine eligibility review

The city's Measure ULA materials discuss exemptions and special treatment for certain circumstances. A seller should not rely on a casual online claim that an entity type, a family relationship or a particular transfer form automatically removes the tax. Property interests and transaction documents can be complex, and the legally applicable facts matter. When an exception may be relevant, the seller's tax adviser and legal counsel should review the current city rules and documentation before those assumptions are built into the transaction.

Planning begins before the property is listed

If a property is likely to fall into a Measure ULA tier, discussing the likely impact early allows pricing expectations, timing and offer strategy to be evaluated with better information. A real estate professional helps the owner understand the marketing plan and likely transaction scenarios, while the relevant professional advisers confirm the legal and tax calculations. That distinction keeps the selling conversation practical and avoids presenting an agent as a substitute for licensed tax or legal guidance.
